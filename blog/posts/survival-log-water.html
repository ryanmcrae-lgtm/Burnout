SURVIVAL LOG 002:
Water Reclamation Under Field Conditions

[ INCOMING TRANSMISSION // KOROB OUTPOST // PRIORITY: STANDARD ]

There is no clean water on Korob. There is only water that will kill you fast and water that will kill you slow.

Experienced operators use reclamation rigs — cobbled together from salvaged condensers and carbon-filtered tank liners. The rigs don't produce good water. They produce survivable water. On Korob, that distinction is the whole of the mission.

// ---- //

THE RESOURCE PROBLEM

Every operator on Korob is working with a finite supply of something that is simultaneously essential and continuously depleted. On the surface, that's water. In the underlying condition the planet represents — burnout — that resource is energy. Specifically, the cognitive and emotional kind that doesn't replenish automatically just because you slept eight hours and drank your coffee.

The mistake most operators make is treating this resource as a performance metric rather than a survival condition. You don't optimise your way to a full tank. You reclaim what you can, from what's available, on a cycle that's shorter than you'd like.

[ FIELD NOTE ]

The operators who last longest on Korob are not the ones who consume the least. They are the ones who reclaim the most efficiently. Conservation alone will not save you. You need active replenishment from low-grade sources.

BUILDING A RECLAMATION RIG

A functional water reclamation rig requires three components. Their non-Korob equivalents are noted:

  • Condenser unit — Something that pulls moisture from the environment rather than waiting for rain. In non-Korob terms: passive restoration. Activities that refill rather than drain. The ones you've probably stopped doing because they feel non-productive.
  • Carbon filter — The mechanism that removes contaminants before the water reaches your system. Equivalent: boundaries. The ability to process incoming demands before they hit your reserves directly. Without this, everything goes straight to the tank.
  • Storage capacity — The ability to hold more than you need in the moment, so you have buffer when sources go dry. Equivalent: rest that goes beyond recovery into actual surplus. Not sleeping until you can function. Sleeping until you want to be awake.

FIELD CONDITIONS THAT COMPROMISE RECLAMATION

Most rigs fail not because of poor design but because of field conditions that were not accounted for:

  • High dust environments (see Sector 7 log): Particulate clogs filters faster. Increase maintenance frequency. Do not wait for the filter to fail to clean it.
  • Extended output phases: Running the rig hard without maintenance intervals degrades all three components simultaneously. The rig will work past its rated capacity. Once. After that, you're in a degraded state with no backup.
  • Social dehydration: Isolation is not the same as rest. An operator alone in a high-pressure environment is not recovering. They are consuming reserves with no input stream. Find people who are not in crisis. Let them be your source.

THE MAINTENANCE SCHEDULE

There is no universal maintenance schedule. Every operator has different capacity and different drain rates. What experienced operators agree on is this: the schedule should exist, it should be non-negotiable, and it should be set when you are resourced — not when you are depleted.

Setting maintenance protocols when you're already running dry produces schedules that are too sparse, too optimistic, and built around the minimum viable survival bar rather than actual replenishment.

Set the schedule now. Before you need it. That's what it's for.

// ---- //

There is no clean water on Korob. But there is water. The operators who make it are the ones who keep the rig running — imperfectly, with salvaged parts, on a cycle that's never quite enough. That's not a failure condition. That's the operational standard. Operate accordingly.
